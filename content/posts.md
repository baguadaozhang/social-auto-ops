We run incoming inspection on every batch of stone that comes into the shop, not just a spot check at the end. For vanity tops the killer is shade variation: two slabs that look identical in the yard can look like different stones under a bathroom light. So we lay the panels out under 5000K light and grade them before cutting.

We also photograph every batch and keep the images with the job file. When a customer in Europe says the second container doesn't match the first, we can pull the photos instead of arguing. #qualitycontrol

Lead time conversations usually go wrong at the sample stage, not in production. We quote 35 days from PO, but that assumes the buyer's sample is approved and the packaging artwork is signed off. If those slip a week, the shipment slips a week, and nobody remembers whose week it was.

So we now put sample approval and artwork approval on the PI as separate dated milestones. It looks fussy on paper, but it ends the "you promised 35 days" conversation before it starts.

For OEM bathroom furniture, the first sample is rarely the problem. It's sample number two. The first one gets made by the senior team with extra care. The second one, made by whoever is free that week, tells you what the factory is actually like.

We now ask for that second sample to be produced during normal production, on the line, not in the sample room. It costs a week and a bit of scrap, but the conversation afterward is much shorter.

Packing is where we lose money quietly. Cartons that pass a drop test in the factory still arrive with crushed corners, because the loading crew stacks three pallets high and the bottom one carries all of it.

Two things changed for us: corner protectors on every mirror carton, and one photo of the loaded container taken from the door side. That photo has ended more claims than any packing specification we have ever written. #exportpacking

CUPC and Watermark are not paperwork exercises, they change the product. A faucet that passes CUPC has a specific aerator and flow restrictor combination, and swapping the cartridge for cost reasons can quietly void the listing.

We learned that the annoying way on a shower system whose original cartridge was discontinued. The replacement worked fine in the lab and failed in the field.

Tolerances are the least glamorous thing we deal with, and buyers usually ask about them last. Wood moves, and a vanity in a humid bathroom moves more. We hold 2 mm on visible joints and 3 mm on hidden ones, and we say so up front.

The factories quoting tighter numbers than that are usually quoting the sample room, not the shipment. It sounds like a small difference until 300 cabinets have to be reworked because the drawer fronts don't line up. #bathroomfurniture
